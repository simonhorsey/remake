Quick Wins

Five study techniques AI can’t do for you

Simon HorseyTeacher and author of Practice Makes Perfect

Why this matters now

An answer you are handed is not an answer you have learned.

AI is amazing. Ask it anything and a clear answer arrives in seconds. That is exactly why knowing how to learn matters more than ever.

To check whether what you are told is right, you need knowledge of your own to compare it with. To think critically, you need something in your head to think with. To be creative, you need ideas you actually own, ready to be joined in new ways.

None of that can be served up. It has to be built.

And building it takes effort. Teachers call it productive struggle: the slightly uncomfortable feeling of trying to remember, explain or work something out for yourself. That feeling is not a sign that you are failing. It is the feeling of learning.

In the exam hall there is no assistant. There is only what you know.

The one idea behind all five

Each technique in this guide makes your brain do the work. It is called active recall: pulling information back out of your memory, not just putting it in front of your eyes. It takes a little more effort. It is worth it.

Quick Wins · LearningForSuccess2
01

Quick win

Put the highlighter down

The problem

Highlighting feels like work. But if you have highlighted nearly everything, you have highlighted nothing. Worse, your brain can decide it knows something simply because you took the action of colouring it in.

Do this

  1. Save the highlighter for the odd word. A term you keep forgetting, a date, a formula. Nothing more.
  2. Write a question in the margin. Each time a section matters, turn it into a question: Why did this happen? How does this work?
  3. Cover the text and answer it. Later, use your margin questions to test yourself with the page hidden.

Why it works

A highlighted line asks nothing of you. A question does. Answering it from memory is the effort that makes the knowledge stick.

Try it tonight

Take one page of notes. Write three questions in the margin, cover the page and answer them out loud.

02

Quick win

Read. Close. Write.

The problem

Reading a chapter through and thinking you have learned it will not cut it. The words feel familiar while the book is open. Close it and most of them are gone.

Do this

  1. Read one section. Not the whole chapter: one section.
  2. Close the book. Properly closed, not face down and peeking.
  3. Write a short summary in your own words. Then open the book and see what you missed.

Stuck for what to write? Ask four questions: What? Why? When? How? It works for videos too: watch, stop, write.

Why it works

Summarising forces you to recall and to choose what matters. Both are thinking. Rereading is neither.

Try it tonight

Pick the last thing you studied in any subject. Give yourself one minute to write a summary without looking.

03

Quick win

Take one chapter through SQ3R

The problem

You open the textbook at the first line and read to the last, with no idea what you are looking for. So nothing in particular stays.

Do this

  1. Survey. Skim the headings, diagrams, key words and call-out boxes. Get the view from 5,000 metres.
  2. Question. What do you already know? What do you need to find out? Write your questions down.
  3. Read. Go section by section, about 20 minutes at a time, hunting for the answers.
  4. Recite. Look away and say the key points in your own words. Reread only what would not come back.
  5. Review. At the end, go over the whole chapter and how its ideas connect. Explain it to someone if you can.

Why it works

Questions give your reading a purpose, and reciting is active recall again. It may feel slow at first. It saves time later.

Try it tonight

Find a chapter you have struggled with. Spend 40 minutes taking it through all five steps.

04

Quick win

Set a 25-minute timer

The problem

The job feels too big, so you put it off. Or you sit for two hours with your phone beside you and get twenty minutes of real work done.

Do this

  1. Clear the desk. Notifications off. Phone in another room if you can.
  2. Set a simple timer for 25 minutes. A kitchen timer or your phone’s clock. No special app needed.
  3. Work on one thing until it rings. One subject, one task.
  4. Take a 5-minute break and move: walk, stretch, get outside. After four rounds, take 15 to 30 minutes.

Cannot face 25? Promise yourself five minutes. Starting is the hard part, and you can carry on if you feel like it.

Why it works

Short, focused bursts beat long, distracted sessions. And consistency matters more than motivation: you will not always feel like revising, but a timer gets you going anyway.

Try it tonight

One 25-minute round on the subject you have been avoiding. Tick it off on a calendar and start a streak.

05

Quick win

Sit a past paper for real

The problem

The hardest thing to know is what you do not know. Revising your favourite topics again feels good and tells you nothing.

Do this

  1. Sit the paper under exam conditions. Timed, silent, no notes.
  2. Do not look at it beforehand, and do not peek at the mark scheme. You would only be cheating yourself out of finding the gaps.
  3. Mark it with the mark scheme. Notice how model answers are worded and what earns the marks.
  4. Go back to every wrong answer. Review that topic in your notes, and ask your teacher if you cannot see why you went wrong.

Why it works

Practice makes permanent. A low score on a practice paper is not a low score in the final. It is a map showing exactly where your revision should go next.

Try it this week

Ask a teacher for one past paper and its mark scheme. Book the time in your calendar now.

Where AI fits

Struggle first. Then check.

Use it after you have tried

  • Ask it to quiz you on a topic you have just summarised
  • Ask it to explain the one point you could not recall
  • Ask it to check your answer and show what is missing

Do not let it do the learning

  • Writing your summary for you: the writing is the learning
  • Giving you the answer before you have had a go
  • Being trusted without a second look: compare it with what you know

Your first week

  • Day 1. Write three margin questions on one page of notes and answer them with the page covered.
  • Day 2. Read, close, write on one section of a textbook.
  • Day 3. Take one difficult chapter through SQ3R.
  • Day 4. Do two 25-minute rounds on the subject you have been avoiding.
  • Day 5. Sit one past paper under exam conditions, then mark it.

The effort is not the obstacle to learning. The effort is the learning.
